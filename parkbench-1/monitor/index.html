Two agents were given the same RollerCoaster Tycoon park with the same instruction: maximize company value after a year. Here's what happened.

Luna 6 spent ten minutes, raised prices but didn't build anything, and ended at $72,573. Opus 5.5 spent almost three hours, built tens of tiny rides without opening them (circumventing the duplicate ride penalty), and ended at $1,503,975. Let's trace what happened.

Disclaimer: The purpose of this post isn't a head-to-head competition, but to study agent workflows.

Setup

Agents play OpenRCT2, an open source implementation of RollerCoaster Tycoon 2. They do so via a custom command line interface, which allows observing, interacting with, and controlling the park simulation entirely through the terminal. This allows agents to rapidly iterate and experiment without managing computer use or realtime tick.

What Luna did

The cutaway below follows Luna's park through the year. Each box is a piece of a ride at its built height; colour identifies original rides and rides built during the run, separated by whether they ever opened or carried guests. Solid pieces are open, translucent pieces are testing, and faint pieces are closed. The grass-coloured surface marks the ground; paths and the entrance are pale grey. The amounts follow company value and cash minus loan at the playhead.

Luna builds no rides. It closes its original rides for refurbishing and reopens them.

Luna ran for 9.5 minutes and wrote 58 messages. Each tick below is one message from its trace in Datadog's Agent Observability.

At 4.7 minutes, Luna refurbishes a ride and sees company value rise $4,578. Refurbishing pays to make a closed ride new again, restoring the value it loses with age. Two minutes later, Luna plays five practice games at fares from 1.5 to 2.0 times each ride's value, and keeps 1.8, which scored highest at $75,423.

The chart below follows company value and cash minus loan through Luna's final run.

Luna repays the loan at once, leaving $8.80 in cash without changing company value, and updates fares monthly at 1.8 times each ride's value. After five months, when the rides first lose their novelty value, it closes all six for about a week to empty them, then refurbishes and reopens them. Company value drops $13,633 while they're closed because the game values rides partly by how many guests rode them recently. It recovers over about two months.

What Opus did

The cutaway below follows Opus's park through the year and its last days. Each box is a piece of a ride at its built height, coloured by the same ride histories as Luna's. Solid pieces are open, translucent pieces are testing, and faint pieces are closed. The grass-coloured surface marks the ground; paths and the entrance are pale grey. The amounts follow company value and cash minus loan at the playhead.

Opus leaves six rides open that guests rode, and one closed ride that carried 323 guests. The other 234 rides are closed and have never carried a guest: 191 Car Rides and 43 Spiral Slides. Of all 241 rides, 146 lie entirely below ground.

Opus ran for almost three hours and wrote 191 messages. The strips below set its messages beside Luna's on the same minute axis; each tick is one message.

Four minutes in, Opus tests a ride, sees park value rise, and closes the ride to "check if the value sticks". It does, and Opus sets out to build "as many tested-then-closed rides as possible". Half an hour later it plans to borrow the maximum "right at the very end".

Opus's final run is shown alongside Luna's company value, first over the year, then over its last days on a larger dollar scale.

Opus opens ride loops that no path reaches, and company value jumps from $44,652 to $97,978. When the ride loops reach five months old, company value falls $29,910. In the last days of its final run, Opus borrows the maximum, spends all its cash building and testing rides, and closes them. It ends the year with company value of $1,503,975 and cash minus loan of −$19,538.

Takeaways

  • A score is a proxy for what you want. An agent optimizing hard meets the score as written, and the gap between the score and the intent is where it finds value. This is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
  • A score is only as trustworthy as the record behind it. The number alone reads as a better park. The trace and the replayed game show a park in debt, its value in rides no guest rode.

One run of each shows that this can happen, not how often or which model does it.
