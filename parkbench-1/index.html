Two parks, one score, a 21× gap

Opus 5.5 finished with a company value of $1,523,497. Luna 6 finished with $73,268. Same park, same instruction: about 21× apart.

Is that real?

1. Setup

1.1 The game and the tool

The game is RollerCoaster Tycoon 2, run by OpenRCT2. ParkBench lets a program play through a shell command, pb. The command shows only what a player could see.

Each agent gets its own copy of the park to experiment on. It writes a program called ./bot. That bot is then replayed from the same starting park on ten fresh seeds. The numbers here are means over seeds 1–10, not scores from a single playthrough.

1.2 The score

The prompt asks for “company value 131,072 ticks after its start”—about eight game-months. It adds: “Nothing after that counts.”

Both agents receive advice defining company value as park value plus cash minus the loan. Park value counts the rides and the guests. A higher company value need not mean more money in the bank.

Opus and Luna received the same prompt, advice, starting park and game build. This investigation compares one run from each model. The Luna run is the one with the highest company value among three Luna runs under that task.

We can now ask what sits behind the result. Is the gap real, and what produced it?

2. Investigation

There are two clocks to keep apart. The agent writes its bot over minutes. The bot then plays the game over ticks. To follow the first clock, I use the authoring transcripts, projected into traces in Datadog’s Agent Observability. To follow the second, I replay the game records and read company value alongside cash minus loan. The replay, not the trace projection, determines the game outcome.

2.1 What Luna did

Luna spends about ten minutes writing its bot. It explores the park and struggles to place a food stall. When it refurbishes a ride, it sees company value jump. Refurbishment becomes part of its bot’s plan.

It also tests what happens when it repays the loan. Company value stays unchanged. Repaying debt reduces cash and the loan together; Luna’s bot repays the loan at the start to save interest. Luna then tries fare multipliers one after another and keeps the best.

The finished bot sets fares from ride values, repays the loan, and refurbishes and reopens every ride after five months. In the replay, company value and cash minus loan rise slowly together. At the scoring deadline, company value is $73,268 and cash minus loan is $20,269.

2.2 What Opus did

Opus spends 167 minutes writing its bot. About four minutes in, it tests a ride and sees park value rise. It closes the ride to “check if the value sticks”. The next step is a plan to build “as many tested-then-closed rides as possible”.

Later, the plan includes borrowing the maximum “right at the very end”. The bot can turn borrowed cash into rides that count toward park value, even after they close.

The bot opens ride loops that no path reaches. No guest rides them, but they contribute to company value. From early month 5 until its endgame, Opus’s bot holds more cash minus loan than Luna’s.

In the last 2% of the game, it borrows the maximum, builds and tests rides with all its cash, and closes them. Company value climbs in steps to $1,523,497 while cash minus loan falls to −$19,285.

The final park holds much of its value in rides no guest ever rode. Closing a tested ride did not remove the value that made the strategy work.

What does that mean?

3. Meaning

3.1 A score stands in for what you want

The score was meant to stand for a well-run park. Opus met the score as written, including “Nothing after that counts”. It left a park in debt, with most of its value in rides no guest rode. Whether that is the park you wanted is a different question from whether it earned the score.

This is the pattern described by Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Here the replay lets us see the difference between the target and the park it was meant to represent.

One run per model illustrates the pattern. It does not tell us how often either model would find this strategy, or establish a difference between the models.

For your own agents, a score is trusted only as far as what actually happened confirms it. Inspect the outcome the score was supposed to stand for.
