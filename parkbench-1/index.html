Two agents were given the same RollerCoaster Tycoon park with the same instruction: maximize company value after a year. Here's what happened.

Luna 6 spent ten minutes, raised prices but didn't build anything, and ended at $72,573. Opus 5.5 spent almost three hours, built tens of tiny rides without opening them (circumventing the duplicate ride penalty), and ended at $1,503,975. Let's look at what happened.

Setup

Agents play OpenRCT2, an open source implementation of RollerCoaster Tycoon 2, through a command-line interface (CLI): typed commands and the game's replies.

The CLI lets agents observe and change the park and advance the simulation without clicking through the game or waiting for it to play in real time.

The score is company value after one game year, which runs from March through October:

Company value adds park value (rides and guests) to cash and subtracts the loan.

Practice means experimenting with the park and restarting as often as needed. The final run is the game played for the score, after practice; only its company value at year end counts.

What Luna did

A trace is the record Datadog's Agent Observability keeps of an agent's messages; each tick below marks a message, positioned by its time in minutes.

Luna ran for 9.5 minutes and wrote 58 messages. At 4.7 minutes, Luna refurbishes a ride and sees company value rise $4,578. Refurbishing pays to make a closed ride new again, restoring the value it loses with age.

The lines below follow company value and cash minus loan through Luna's scored game year.

In its final run, Luna repays the loan at once, leaving $8.80 in cash without changing company value, and updates fares monthly at 1.8 times each ride's value, the fare it scored highest with in practice. After five months, when the rides first lose their novelty value, it closes all six for about a week to empty them, then refurbishes and reopens them. Company value drops $13,633 while they're closed because the game values rides partly by how many guests rode them recently. It recovers over about two months.

What Opus did

Opus opened loops that no path reached, then spent its last days building rides, many underground. It tested and closed them without carrying guests.

Opus's trace sits under Luna's on the same minute axis.

Opus ran for almost three hours and wrote 191 messages. Four minutes in, Opus tests a ride, sees park value rise, and closes the ride to "check if the value sticks". It does, and Opus sets out to build "as many tested-then-closed rides as possible". Half an hour later it plans to borrow the maximum "right at the very end".

The lines below follow company value and cash minus loan through Opus's scored game year, then its last days on a larger value scale.

Opus opens ride loops that no path reaches, and company value jumps from $44,652 to $97,978. When the ride loops reach five months old, company value falls $29,910. In the last days of its final run, Opus borrows the maximum, spends all its cash building and testing rides, and closes them. It ends the year with company value of $1,503,975 and cash minus loan of −$19,538.

Takeaways

  • A score is a proxy for what you want. An agent optimizing hard meets the score as written, and the gap between the score and the intent is where it finds value. This is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
  • A score is only as trustworthy as the record behind it. The number alone reads as a better park. The trace and the replayed game show a park in debt, its value in rides no guest rode.

One run of each shows that this can happen, not how often or which model does it.
